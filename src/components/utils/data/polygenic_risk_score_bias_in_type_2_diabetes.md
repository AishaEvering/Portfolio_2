# Polygenic Risk Score Bias in Type 2 Diabetes

_A Research Project in Computational Genomics, Machine Learning, and Health Equity_

## Overview

This project explores how ancestry, population structure, and genomic variation influence the performance of Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) for Type 2 Diabetes prediction across diverse populations.

Polygenic Risk Scores are increasingly used in precision medicine to estimate an individual’s genetic predisposition to disease. However, many PRS models are primarily trained on datasets from European populations, raising concerns about predictive bias and reduced performance in underrepresented groups.

This project investigates the scientific, computational, and ethical challenges associated with applying PRS models across genetically diverse populations.

---

## Research Focus

The project examined several major factors affecting PRS transferability and fairness, including:

- Population structure
- Linkage disequilibrium differences
- Allele frequency variation
- Dataset imbalance
- Predictive bias in genomic machine learning models
- Reduced model transferability across ancestries

The research also explored how these limitations may impact healthcare equity and precision medicine initiatives.

## Models & Methods Reviewed

This project analyzed and compared several emerging multi-ancestry PRS approaches, including:

- LDpred2
- PRS-CSx
- MUSSEL
- PolyPred
- FairPRS
- DisPred
- PhyloFrame

The review examined how these methods attempt to improve PRS performance and reduce ancestry-related disparities in genomic prediction.

---

## Key Insights

- PRS performance often declines significantly in non-European populations due to differences in linkage disequilibrium and allele frequency patterns.
- Dataset imbalance remains one of the largest barriers to equitable genomic prediction.
- Multi-ancestry modeling approaches show promise, but many challenges remain unresolved.
- Machine learning methods may improve predictive performance, but they can also reinforce existing biases if training data remains unbalanced.
- Precision medicine requires both computational advancement and representative genomic datasets to achieve equitable outcomes.
