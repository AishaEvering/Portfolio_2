## Organism

Anaeramoeba flamelloides is a free-living, anaerobic marine amoeboflagellate
found in anoxic shallow marine sediments, including sandy and muddy
environments.

It belongs to:

Metamonada → Anaeramoebae → Anaeramoebidae → Anaeramoeba

A. flamelloides primarily exhibits an amoeboid form but can transition into a
flagellated form. Its morphology changes depending on cellular activity,
including locomotive, non-directly moving, and floating forms.

## Phylogenetic Position

Anaeramoebae is a phylum-level lineage within Metamonada and is the sister
group to Parabasalia.

The phylogenomic analysis discussed in the project used:

- 95 taxa
- 155 proteins
- 53,637 aligned sites
- 1,000 bootstrap replicates

Adding Anaeramoebae to the phylogeny helped break up the long branch leading
to parabasalids and improved understanding of deep relationships within
Metamonada.

A. flamelloides is particularly interesting because its cellular morphology
differs considerably from the elaborate flagellated morphology typical of
many parabasalids despite their phylogenetic relationship.

## Hydrogenosomes

A. flamelloides contains hundreds of hydrogenosomes.

Two reconstructed cells described in the literature contained 374 and 307
hydrogenosomes, accounting for approximately 6% of total cell volume.

Hydrogenosomes are mitochondrion-related organelles rather than classical
aerobic mitochondria.

The hydrogenosomes of A. flamelloides retain mitochondrial characteristics
that provide insight into the evolutionary transition from mitochondria to
hydrogenosomes.

## The Symbiosome

One of the most unusual features of A. flamelloides is its intracellular
relationship with sulfate-reducing bacterial symbionts related to the family
Desulfobacteraceae.

The bacteria are housed inside a specialized membrane-bound structure called
the symbiosome.

More recent 3D reconstructions showed that this structure is not a collection
of separate vesicles. It is a single interconnected membrane compartment.

In the cell reconstruction highlighted on Aisha's poster:

- 374 hydrogenosomes were positioned closely along the symbiosome membrane
- The hydrogenosomes occupied approximately 6.3% of cell volume
- The interconnected symbiosome housed 160 Desulfobacter-related symbionts
- The symbiosome occupied approximately 15.3% of the cell's volume

## Why Are There So Many Hydrogenosomes?

The close physical association between the hydrogenosomes and bacterial
symbionts suggests metabolic cooperation.

Hydrogenosomes release metabolic products including molecular hydrogen (H₂)
and acetate.

These compounds may be used by the sulfate-reducing bacterial symbionts.

The relationship can therefore be summarized as:

Hydrogenosomes → H₂ + acetate → sulfate-reducing symbionts

The symbiosome also contains membrane-lined pores that may allow the
intracellular bacteria to access sulfate from the surrounding seawater.

Together, these observations are consistent with a syntrophic metabolic
relationship between A. flamelloides and its bacterial symbionts.

## Why This Organism Is Interesting

A. flamelloides combines several unusual biological features:

- An anaerobic lifestyle
- Amoeboid and flagellated forms
- Hundreds of hydrogenosomes
- A giant interconnected intracellular symbiosome
- Sulfate-reducing bacterial symbionts
- An unusual phylogenetic position within Metamonada

These features make it useful for studying mitochondrial evolution,
anaerobic eukaryotic metabolism, microbial symbiosis, and the evolution of
Metamonada.

## Poster Take-Home Message

A. flamelloides is an anoxic amoeba found in marine sands and muds that
houses sulfate-reducing bacterial symbionts within an extensive symbiosome
closely associated with hundreds of hydrogenosomes.

This unusual intracellular architecture is consistent with syntrophic
metabolic exchange between the host and its bacterial symbionts.
