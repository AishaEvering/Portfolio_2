Dmc1 Phylogenetic Research — Aisha Evering
Project overview
Project: Evolution of Dmc1 Homologs Across Metamonada
Institution: Arizona State University, School of Life Sciences
Context: Microbial Eukaryotes Online Research Experience (ORE), 2026
Research area: Computational biology, evolutionary genomics, phylogenetics, homologous recombination

Aisha investigated the phylogenetic distribution of Dmc1 homologs across Metamonada, a diverse group of predominantly anaerobic protists. Dmc1 is a recombinase involved in meiotic homologous recombination. Previous work had reported Dmc1 duplication in Fornicata in association with the loss of Rad51A. The project investigated whether the distribution of Dmc1 homologs across other metamonad lineages suggested a more complex history of duplication, retention, and loss.

This work was part of a three-person collaborative research project, documented in the group manuscript Comparative Phylogenetic Analysis of Mnd1, Hop2, and Dmc1 Across Metamonada, with Aisha Evering, Chloe Nguyen, and Percy Raggi. The team compared three proteins involved in homologous recombination: Dmc1, Mnd1, and Hop2.

  Research question and hypothesis
  The Dmc1 investigation asked how Dmc1 homologs are distributed among metamonad lineages and whether the observed patterns were consistent with gene duplications and losses beyond Fornicata. The broader team hypothesized that Dmc1, Hop2, and Mnd1 might share evolutionary patterns because of their functional roles in meiotic homologous recombination, potentially in association with Dmc1 duplication and Rad51A loss. This was a hypothesis, not a demonstrated conclusion.
  Computational workflow

1. Started with Dmc1 homologs from orthogroup OG6_100483, retrieved through the EukPhylo workflow. The group manuscript explains that earlier transcriptome assembly and initial EukPhylo processing preceded the team's subsequent analysis.
2. Inspected the initial Dmc1 sequence set and phylogeny for redundant sequences. Used Taxus to prune redundant non-metamonad sequences, retaining the better-covered sequence where appropriate.
3. Reduced the Dmc1 dataset from 1,222 to 1,131 sequences, removing 91 redundant sequences.
4. Realigned sequences with MAFFT and trimmed the alignment with trimAl (-automated1).
5. Reconstructed a maximum-likelihood phylogeny with IQ-TREE 2, using the LG+F+G4 substitution model and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates.
6. Visualized the phylogeny with FigTree, and prepared figures for presentation using Adobe Illustrator. The manuscript reports analyses on Arizona State University's Sol HPC cluster.
7. Highlighted major metamonad groups in the Dmc1 tree: Fornicata (teal), Parabasalia (blue), BaSk (olive), Anaeramoebae (orange), Preaxostyla / Paratrimastix and Trimastix (red), and Preaxostyla / Oxymonadida (magenta). Displayed ultrafast bootstrap support values of 95 or higher in the figure.
   Findings

- Dmc1 homologs appeared in multiple distinct regions of the 1,131-sequence gene phylogeny rather than in a single monophyletic metamonad group.
- Fornicata homologs appeared in multiple separated regions, consistent with previously reported multiple Dmc1 copies.
- Multiple Dmc1 homologs were also recovered in BaSk and Preaxostyla/Oxymonadida, indicating that patterns of multiple copies were not restricted to Fornicata.
- Parabasalia appeared in more than one region, while Anaeramoebae homologs clustered in a single region of the Dmc1 tree.
- These observations suggest a potentially more complicated history of Dmc1 duplication and differential retention or loss across Metamonada than a single Fornicata-specific duplication.
- Important limitation: The phylogeny alone does not establish the exact number, timing, or mechanism of duplication events. Some deeper relationships had weak support, sampling was limited, and the Dmc1 tree was not assigned a biological root. Do not present gene duplication scenarios as definitively proven.
  Findings from the collaborative comparison
  The manuscript compared Dmc1 with the Mnd1 and Hop2 trees prepared as part of the team project. Mnd1 and Hop2 showed some apparent similarities, but the phylogenies were not sufficient to establish that the proteins co-evolved. The overall conclusion was that the three functionally related proteins did not necessarily share a single evolutionary history within Metamonada. These are team-level results, not three separate analyses personally performed by Aisha.
